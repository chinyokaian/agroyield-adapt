Abstract—Models that predict crop yield are increasingly published with prediction intervals, and those intervals are increasingly used to support fertiliser, credit and insurance decisions. Three research strands bear on whether such an interval can be trusted in a new region: how the interval is constructed, how the evaluation is designed, and how the model is transported. Reviews normally treat these strands separately. This paper synthesises fifteen primary sources along those three axes and reports four findings. Interval construction is mature, with finite-sample guarantees available under stated assumptions. Evaluation design materially inflates reported skill when spatial or hierarchical structure is ignored, and that inflation is documented but seldom audited in agronomic studies. The standard pre-deployment support check reasons about covariates only and therefore cannot detect a shift in the outcome distribution. Finally, no source reports construction, evaluation and transport for one domain in one place, and none costs the remedy. The review concludes that the missing artefact is a joint audit that separates skill from calibration, diagnoses which shift is responsible, and states the label budget required to restore coverage. Five research gaps follow from the synthesis.

Keywords—conformal prediction; uncertainty quantification; crop yield; distribution shift; spatial cross-validation; data leakage

I. INTRODUCTION

Yield prediction models built on field-level survey data are now common in agronomic research, and they are increasingly reported with prediction intervals rather than point estimates alone. The reason is practical: an interval can be attached to a decision. A fertiliser recommendation, a credit limit or an insurance premium needs to know how uncertain a prediction is, not merely what the prediction is.

An interval is useful only if it is valid where it is used. Validity here has a precise meaning: over repeated draws, the interval should contain the true value at the stated rate. Conformal prediction supplies that property under an assumption of exchangeability, and the framework has become the default way to attach a guarantee to an arbitrary point predictor [1], [2]. The guarantee, however, is marginal: it holds on average over the data used, not for each region or each subgroup, and it can fail when the target data are not exchangeable with the calibration data.

That failure mode matters in agronomy because models are routinely fitted on a multi-country panel and then applied to one country. Three literatures touch this problem without meeting. The conformal literature studies how to construct intervals and what they guarantee [1]–[5]. The validation literature studies how evaluation design inflates reported skill when data are spatially or hierarchically structured [11]–[13]. The spatial-prediction literature studies whether a target location lies inside the predictor's support [10]. A practitioner holding a model therefore has three separate answers and no single verdict.

This paper reviews those strands together. Its contribution is a synthesis organised by three questions: how intervals are constructed, how their validity is evaluated, and how they behave when transported. Section II states the review protocol. Sections III to V treat the three strands in turn. Section VI synthesises them in one table, Section VII states the research gaps that follow, and Section VIII concludes.

II. REVIEW PROTOCOL

A. Review questions

The review addresses three questions. RQ1: what guarantees do the main interval-construction methods provide, and under which assumptions? RQ2: how do evaluation designs affect reported predictive skill, and are interval studies designed to avoid that effect? RQ3: what evidence exists on the behaviour of intervals when a model is moved to a new region, and what remedies are proposed?

B. Sources and search strategy

Searches cover Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar and arXiv, supplemented by the Zenodo record for the dataset used in the associated project. The search combines three term groups: interval and uncertainty terms (conformal prediction, quantile regression, prediction interval, coverage), crop terms (maize, yield, smallholder, agronomic), and transfer terms (distribution shift, covariate shift, applicability, spatial cross-validation, transferability). Reference lists of retained papers are screened for further sources.

C. Inclusion and exclusion

Studies are included when they propose or evaluate a method for predictive uncertainty, or quantify the effect of validation design on reported skill, or assess the transportability of a spatial prediction model. Studies are excluded when they report point accuracy only and make no claim about uncertainty or transport, when the outcome is not a continuous agronomic quantity, and when no method or evaluation is described. No date restriction is applied; the corpus spans 2001 to 2024.

D. Corpus status and data extraction

Fifteen sources are retained for synthesis at the time of writing, and they are the sources cited in this paper; screening against the protocol above is continuing and the final screening counts will be reported in the submitted version rather than estimated here. For each source the following are extracted: the method, the guarantee claimed, the assumption the guarantee rests on, the evaluation design used, whether any distribution shift is handled, and whether a remedy and its cost are stated. These six fields are what Table I reports.

E. Limitations of the review

The review is purposive rather than exhaustive at this stage, it covers one crop, and it relies on the assumptions stated by each source rather than re-deriving them. Where a source is known to exist but its bibliographic details could not be confirmed, it is not cited; the associated workbook records those separately. This is a deliberate choice: an unverified citation is worse than an absent one.

III. CONSTRUCTING AN INTERVAL

Two families dominate. The first estimates conditional quantiles directly, either by quantile regression forests, which read quantiles from the empirical distribution held in the leaves of an ensemble [5], so that the point predictor and the interval come from one model, or by conformalised quantile regression, which uses estimated quantiles as a conformity score and then calibrates the score on held-out data [4]. The second family wraps an arbitrary point predictor, such as a random forest [6]. Split conformal prediction fits the model on one part of the data, computes nonconformity scores on a second part, and takes a quantile of those scores as the interval half-width [3]. Its appeal is that the guarantee does not depend on the model being correct, only on the calibration and test data being exchangeable [1], [2].

The guarantee is the crux. Split conformal gives marginal coverage: averaged over the test distribution, the interval contains the truth at the nominal rate. It does not give coverage conditional on a region, a country or a covariate value, and the difference between those two statements is exactly the difference between a model that is valid on average across a seven-country panel and one that is valid in the country where it will be used. Group-conditional variants relax the assumption by calibrating within groups, at the cost of needing enough data in each group; the tutorial treatment in [1] is explicit that conditional coverage is hard in general.

Under distribution shift the guarantee degrades, and the degradation is quantifiable: coverage-gap bounds are available once the shift is expressed through an appropriate measure [7]. That result is important for practice because it converts an assumed guarantee into a measurable quantity, which is the first step towards reporting a gap rather than asserting a property. What the literature does not supply is a routine way to compute that measure for an agronomic panel, which is why the applied studies in this corpus report nominal coverage and stop there.

IV. EVALUATING THE INTERVAL: DESIGN AND LEAKAGE

An interval is only as credible as the evaluation that produced it. When observations are spatially or hierarchically structured, random cross-validation places near-duplicate records in both training and test sets, and the model's apparent skill includes its ability to recognise those duplicates. The validation literature documents this for ecological and spatial data and prescribes blocked designs instead [11]. The effect is not small: a comparison of random and spatial validation on large-scale mapping models found that spatial validation revealed markedly poorer performance than random validation had suggested [12].

The same problem has a name and a taxonomy. Leakage is the use, during training or evaluation, of information that would not be available at prediction time; a survey across modelling fields catalogues the recurring forms, including duplicate records, temporal leakage and the reuse of test data for model selection [13]. This matters for interval work specifically, because the calibration set is part of the evaluation. If the calibration set shares clusters with the test set, the scores are computed on data the model has effectively seen, and the resulting interval is over-confident by construction — even when every individual step follows the conformal recipe correctly.

The literature reviewed here is uneven on this point. Method papers are careful about exchangeability in principle [1], [3], [7] but do not audit the evaluation design of the applications they motivate; application papers report random or unspecified splits and treat the guarantee as inherited. No source in this corpus reports both a leakage-aware evaluation design and an interval-validity result for the same model, which is the observation that motivates gap G1 in Section VII.

V. TRANSPORT: APPLICABILITY, SHIFT AND REMEDY

Three remedies appear in the corpus, and they answer different questions. The first asks whether the target is inside the predictor's support. The dissimilarity index computes a scaled distance in predictor space from each target location to the training data and flags those beyond a threshold, giving an area of applicability [10]. In agronomic practice this is the standard pre-deployment check, and the source publication of the public field-level panel used in the associated project applies a check of this kind on that panel [14], [15]. Its limitation is structural: it measures the covariates only. A target region can sit comfortably inside the covariate support and still have a different yield distribution, because management, market access or measurement practice differ.

The second remedy reweights the calibration set towards the target distribution. Under covariate shift, weighting the conformity scores by an estimated likelihood ratio restores validity [8]. The method is principled and its failure mode is well understood: the weights concentrate on few calibration points, the effective sample size falls, and the resulting interval becomes unstable. This is not a hypothetical concern in a seven-country panel, where one country can be an outlier in several covariates at once.

The third remedy assumes the shift is in the outcome distribution rather than the covariates, and adapts the threshold accordingly. Methods for tracking a deployed model's risk under label shift establish the principle that the score distribution, not only the covariate distribution, carries the information needed to correct coverage [9]. Applied to yield, this suggests that a small labelled sample from the target region, used to estimate the level offset, may be more informative than a large unlabelled sample and a covariate diagnostic. The corpus does not test that proposition, and it does not quantify how many labels such a correction needs — the quantity a deployment decision actually depends on.

Read across Table I, the pattern is not a gap in any single row but a gap between rows. Each family answers its own question correctly. None of them reports, for one domain, both the honest point skill and the interval validity, and none states what it would cost to repair the interval. The practitioner's question — is this model safe here, and what would make it safe — falls between the columns.

Gap G3 deserves emphasis because it is the one a deployment decision turns on. A remedy that restores coverage by demanding two thousand labelled observations in every target region is not a remedy for a smallholder extension service. Stating the budget turns a statistical correction into an operational choice, and no source in this corpus states it.

VIII. CONCLUSION

The three strands reviewed here are individually mature and jointly incomplete. Interval construction is settled enough to be applied routinely; evaluation design is known to inflate reported skill when structure is ignored; and transport is handled either by a covariate-space support check or by a reweighting scheme whose failure mode is known. What does not exist in the corpus is an instrument that puts the three together for one domain, states which of them is failing, and costs the repair. That absence is the gap this review identifies, and it is the gap the associated capstone project addresses by building and evaluating a deployment-readiness auditor for smallholder maize yield models.
