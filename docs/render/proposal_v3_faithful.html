ABSTRACT

Smallholder maize yield models are increasingly published with prediction intervals, and those intervals are used to justify fertiliser, credit and crop-insurance decisions. Two failures recur in this literature and are usually reported separately: reported point accuracy is inflated by the design used to evaluate it, and interval coverage collapses when a model is applied to a region it was not calibrated on. This project develops AGROYIELD-ADAPT, a deployment-readiness auditor that reports both failures together, identifies which region is unsafe, and prescribes a calibration remedy together with the label and interval-width cost of that remedy. The artefact is a tested Python tool with five modules: split integrity, skill and calibration separation, transport diagnosis, level-adjusted recalibration, and report generation. It is evaluated on the One Acre Fund smallholder maize panel, a public dataset of 14,773 field-season records from seven countries covering 2016 to 2022, licensed CC BY 4.0, using random, spatially clustered and leave-one-country-out validation against pooled, per-domain and label-free calibration baselines. The first module is complete and unit-tested: the same random forest scores an R-squared of +0.265 under random five-fold validation, +0.177 when spatial-temporal clusters are held out, and -1.943 when whole countries are held out, an inflation of +0.088 and +2.208 R-squared respectively. Preliminary results indicate that coverage error is monotone in the target domain's yield level while the standard dissimilarity-index check is uncorrelated with it, a hypothesis the remaining modules will test at stratum level. The auditor is intended for analysts who must decide whether a yield model is safe to deploy in a particular region.

Keywords: conformal prediction; interval calibration; distribution shift; smallholder maize; model auditing; data leakage

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background and problem statement

Models that predict smallholder maize yield are increasingly reported with prediction intervals, and those intervals are increasingly used to justify decisions about fertiliser recommendation, credit and crop insurance. Two failures recur in this literature and are usually reported separately: reported point accuracy is inflated by evaluation design, and interval coverage collapses when a model is moved to a new region. A practitioner receiving such a model today has no instrument that reports both problems together, states which region is unsafe, and costs the remedy.

This project addresses that gap by building, testing and evaluating a software artefact – a deployment-readiness auditor – and by using it to measure how far common calibration strategies actually travel across smallholder maize domains in Sub-Saharan Africa.

1.2 Aim

The aim is to develop and reproducibly evaluate a deployment-readiness auditor that diagnoses split inflation and interval miscalibration in maize yield models, and prescribes a calibration remedy with its label and interval-width cost.

1.3 Objectives

O1. Establish how much reported skill is an artefact of validation design for a multinational smallholder maize panel, using random, spatially clustered and leave-one-country-out validation with identical features and model.

O2. Separate model skill from interval validity: report point-transfer accuracy and empirical coverage per domain as independent verdicts.

O3. Identify which measurable properties of a target domain predict coverage loss, and test whether the field's standard applicability diagnostic detects it.

O4. Implement a level-adjusted calibration procedure with a label-budget rule, and evaluate it against pooled, per-domain and label-free weighting baselines.

O5. Deliver the auditor as documented, tested software with a reproducibility record, and evaluate its warning behaviour.

1.4 Research questions and hypotheses

RQ1. How large is the inflation in reported predictive skill when spatial and national structure are respected?

RQ2. Do intervals calibrated on a multi-country pool retain nominal coverage in a held-out country, and does point-model skill predict whether they will?

RQ3. Is coverage loss predictable from observable properties of the target domain, and does the dissimilarity-index applicability check detect it?

RQ4. What label budget and interval-width cost does restoring coverage require?

Four pre-declared hypotheses follow, each falsifiable and each reported whichever way it falls: H1 inflation exceeds 0.05 R-squared on the clustered design; H2 pooled calibration does not reach nominal coverage in at least one domain and coverage is not monotone in point-model skill; H3 coverage loss is associated with the target domain's yield level rather than with covariate-space dissimilarity alone; H4 local recalibration restores coverage at no more than a stated width penalty.

1.5 Scope and delimitation

In scope: a Python command-line auditor with five modules (split integrity, skill and calibration separation, transport diagnosis, level-adjusted recalibration, report generation), evaluated on one public panel.

Out of scope, and not claimed: a new theory of conformal prediction; live data ingestion; a mobile application; a commercial backend; causal effects of changing agronomic practice; farmer income or insurance outcomes; national representativeness; and validity for Zimbabwe, which the dataset does not cover. The contribution is an instrument plus measured evidence, not a new algorithm.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW AND EVIDENCE BASE

2.1 The dataset and its provenance

The study uses the One Acre Fund smallholder maize panel (Aramburu Merlos et al., 2024), deposited on Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.11122388) under CC BY 4.0 and used in the dataset's own source publication (Aramburu-Merlos et al., 2024). The local copy has been audited and fingerprinted: 14,773 field-season records, 53 columns, seven countries (Kenya 7,258; Tanzania 3,710; Rwanda 2,080; Zambia 861; Burundi 640; Nigeria 115; Uganda 109), survey years 2016–2020 and 2022, 263 zero-yield records (1.78%), 1,526 spatial-temporal clusters at 0.1 degree plus year, and 4,764 records (32.2%) sharing a cluster with another record. Soil covariates are missing for 78.4% of Burundi records and 76.3% of Rwandan records, a limitation carried forward to the analysis.

2.2 Related work and positioning

Split conformal prediction gives marginal coverage under exchangeability (Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer, 2005; Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023), and its coverage guarantee is known to degrade under distribution shift, with bounds available once the shift is quantified (Barber et al., 2023). Conditioning coverage on a domain, as Mondrian conformal prediction does, restores validity at the cost of wider intervals. Two families of remedy appear in the literature: reweighting the calibration set towards the target distribution (Tibshirani et al., 2019) and recalibrating locally on labelled target data (Lei et al., 2018; Podkopaev and Ramdas, 2021).

Separately, the spatial-prediction literature offers the dissimilarity index and area of applicability as a pre-deployment check on whether a target location is inside the predictor's support (Meyer and Pebesma, 2021). That check reasons about covariates only. Finally, a substantial methodological literature documents how evaluation design inflates reported skill through leakage (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023), which is directly relevant to a panel in which 32.2% of records share a location and year with another record.

The gap this project addresses is that these three strands are reported separately: point accuracy, interval validity and applicability are rarely presented as one verdict for one domain, and the cost of the remedy is almost never stated. The contribution is therefore an instrument that joins them and measures the cost, not a new theory.

CHAPTER 3: RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

3.1 Artefact design

The artefact is developed as five modules, each independently testable:

M1 Split integrity. Fits one model under random, cluster-grouped and leave-one-country-out validation and reports the resulting inflation.

M2 Skill and calibration separation. Reports point-transfer accuracy and empirical interval coverage per domain as separate verdicts.

M3 Transport diagnosis. Computes dissimilarity index and out-of-area share, prediction-distribution distance, effective sample size of density-ratio weights, and the target domain's yield-level offset, then issues a pass, warn or fail flag.

M4 Level-adjusted recalibration. Corrects the score quantile for the target domain's level offset from a small labelled sample and reports the label budget required.

M5 Report generation. Produces a machine-readable and human-readable readiness report.

3.2 Evaluation design

Evaluation uses three validation designs, three seeds and a second model family. Pooled calibration is compared with per-domain (Mondrian) calibration, label-free density-ratio weighting and the level-adjusted procedure of M4, at a nominal coverage of 0.90. The auditor's own warning behaviour is assessed against observed coverage loss, so the tool is evaluated as software as well as used as an instrument. Effect sizes are reported with their uncertainty, and fold-seeds are never treated as independent observations.

3.3 Reproducibility and data handling

All preprocessing is fold-internal: imputation statistics are estimated on training folds only, because global imputation silently uses test-set statistics. Every run is recorded with its seeds, fold assignments and a checksum of the data file, and code is versioned in a public repository. An earlier run that imputed globally is retained as a documented sensitivity case rather than discarded.

4.2 Calibration pilot

Preliminary work from the development pilot, not yet recomputed under the auditor's preprocessing, suggests the calibration picture: pooled calibration at nominal 0.90 gives empirical coverage between 0.574 and 1.000 across the seven countries; per-domain calibration gives 0.852 to 0.930 at a median width of 0.95 times pooled; label-free density-ratio weighting does not improve on pooled calibration and retains an effective sample size of only 0.09 to 0.17. Across the seven domains the signed coverage error is monotone in the domain's median yield (Spearman rho = +0.96, n = 7) and uncorrelated with the dissimilarity index (rho = 0.00).

4.3 Discussion

With seven domains this evidence is suggestive, not conclusive, and it is reported as a hypothesis under test rather than as a finding. If it survives replication at stratum level it would imply that pre-deployment support checks based on covariates cannot detect the failure that matters for intervals, and that a small labelled sample from the target domain is the only reliable remedy — which is precisely the cost the auditor is designed to state.

CHAPTER 5: CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

5.1 Summary

The project delivers an instrument and a body of measured evidence about how maize yield-interval models fail when moved between regions, and what repairing them costs.

5.2 Contribution

The contribution is threefold: an executable audit that joins split integrity, skill and calibration; measured evidence on the size of both failures in a seven-country panel; and a recalibration procedure with an explicit label budget. It is an instrument plus evidence, not a new algorithm, and it is presented as such.

5.3 Limitations

Seven country domains give limited statistical power; a single model family would leave the diagnosis open to a configuration objection; and soil covariates are missing for most records in two countries. Each limitation is addressed by the evaluation design rather than argued away.

5.4 Recommendations and further work

Further work should replicate the diagnosis at stratum level, extend the auditor to a second crop and to seasonal forecast covariates, and test whether the label budget it prescribes is affordable in an extension setting.
