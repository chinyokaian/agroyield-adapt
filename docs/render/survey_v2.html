A Survey of Uncertainty Quantification and Transfer Validation for Smallholder Crop Yield Prediction

IAN CHINYOKA R231473B

Department of Analytics and Informatics, Faculty of Computer Engineering Informatics and Communications

Supervisor: MR ZIMOWA

Abstract—Yield prediction models built on smallholder survey data are increasingly published with prediction intervals, and those intervals increasingly inform fertiliser, credit and insurance decisions. Whether an interval can be trusted in a region it was not calibrated for depends on three separate bodies of work: how the interval is constructed, how the fit is judged, and how the model is moved. This paper surveys fifteen foundational works across those three strands and assesses them against the needs of the base project, a deployment-readiness auditor for smallholder maize yield models. Four findings emerge. Interval construction is mature, with finite-sample guarantees available under stated assumptions. Evaluation design materially inflates reported skill when spatial or hierarchical structure is ignored, and interval studies seldom audit this. The standard pre-deployment support check reasons about covariates only and therefore cannot detect a shift in the outcome distribution. No surveyed work reports construction, honest evaluation and transport for one domain together, and none costs the remedy. The paper concludes that the missing artefact is a joint audit that separates skill from calibration, diagnoses which shift is responsible and states the label budget required to restore coverage, and it sets out five research directions that follow.

Index Terms—conformal prediction; uncertainty quantification; crop yield; distribution shift; spatial cross-validation; data leakage

1. INTRODUCTION

Yield prediction models fitted to field-level survey data are now common in agronomic research, and they are increasingly reported with prediction intervals rather than point estimates alone. The reason is practical: an interval can be attached to a decision. A fertiliser recommendation, a credit limit or an insurance premium needs to know how uncertain a prediction is, not only what the prediction is.

An interval is useful only if it is valid where it is used. Validity has a precise meaning: over repeated draws the interval should contain the true value at the stated rate. Conformal prediction supplies that property under an assumption of exchangeability, and the framework has become the standard way to attach a guarantee to an arbitrary point predictor [1], [2]. The guarantee is marginal, however: it holds on average over the data used, not for each region or each subgroup, and it fails when target data are not exchangeable with calibration data.

That failure mode matters in agronomy because models are routinely fitted on a multi-country panel and then applied to one country. Three literatures bear on the problem without meeting. The conformal literature studies how to construct intervals and what they guarantee [1]–[5]. The validation literature studies how evaluation design inflates reported skill when data are spatially or hierarchically structured [11]–[13]. The spatial-prediction literature studies whether a target location lies inside the predictor's support [10]. A practitioner holding a model therefore has three separate answers and no single verdict.

This paper surveys those strands together and assesses each source against the requirements of the base project, AGROYIELD-ADAPT, a deployment-readiness auditor that must decide whether a smallholder maize yield-interval model is safe to deploy in a particular country. Section 2 states the problem and the base project. Section 3 reviews the fifteen foundational papers, groups them by the question each answers, gives a critical commentary on each, and tabulates what each contributes and what it leaves undone. Section 4 assesses the base project critically. Section 5 sets out the improvements the survey supports and their feasibility. Section 6 concludes and states future directions.

2. PROBLEM DESCRIPTION AND THE BASE PROJECT

The base project addresses a specific and common situation. A model is fitted to the One Acre Fund smallholder maize panel, a public field-level dataset of 14,773 season records from seven Sub-Saharan African countries covering 2016 to 2022 [14]. The panel is observational, unbalanced across countries and years, and contains 4,764 duplicate rows, 32.2 per cent of the file. The model is a random forest [6] fitted to predict maize yield from management and environment variables. The question the project must answer is not how accurate that model is on the panel, but whether an interval produced from it can be trusted when the model is applied to one country, and what it would cost to make it trustworthy.

Three difficulties make that question hard, and each has a literature behind it. First, the dataset is duplicated and clustered, so a random split lets the model see near-copies of test rows during training; the reported accuracy is then partly a measure of duplicate recognition [11], [12]. Second, once the split is honest, the point model's skill and the interval's calibration become two distinct quantities that can fail independently; a model can transfer its ranking ability to a new country while its intervals collapse, and reporting a single number hides that [1], [3]. Third, moving to one country changes both the covariate distribution and the outcome distribution, and the usual pre-deployment check measures only the first [10].

The base project's own preliminary result shows why this matters. The same random forest scores an R-squared of +0.265 under random five-fold validation, +0.177 when spatial-temporal clusters are held out, and −1.943 when whole countries are held out — an inflation of +0.088 and +2.208 respectively. A practitioner who validated the model randomly would conclude it transfers; the honest design says it does not. Because each of the fifteen sources below addresses one part of this picture, reviewing them together is the only way to see which part the base project is missing.

3.1 Constructing an interval and what the guarantee means

Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer [2] established the framework on which everything here rests: a prediction set is valid if, over repeated draws, it fails to contain the truth at most a known rate, and that property follows from the exchangeability of the data rather than from the correctness of the model. For the base project this is the single most useful idea in the corpus, because it explains why an interval can be attached to a random forest whose functional form nobody believes. The limitation is equally important: a textbook treatment assumes exchangeability and offers no guidance for a panel whose rows are grouped by country and year, which is exactly the base project's situation.

Angelopoulos and Bates [1] turn that framework into a recipe. Split conformal prediction fits the model on one part of the data, computes nonconformity scores on a second part and takes a quantile of those scores as the interval half-width. The survey is valuable to the base project for two reasons: it states the recipe precisely enough to implement, and it is candid that conditional coverage — coverage for a given region or covariate value — is hard in general. That candour is the gap the base project must measure rather than assume away: a marginal guarantee across seven countries says nothing about the one country where a recommendation will be issued.

Lei et al. [3] give the finite-sample justification for the split construction in regression, with an explicit marginal coverage statement. Its significance for the base project is procedural: it settles that calibration must be done on data held out from fitting, which rules out the resubstitution habit of quoting in-sample residuals as uncertainty. The assumption is independent and identically distributed sampling, and the paper does not examine what happens when rows are clustered, so the base project cannot inherit the guarantee merely by citing it.

Romano, Patterson and Candès [4] replace the constant-width interval with a conformalised quantile regression, calibrating estimated conditional quantiles so that the interval adapts to heteroscedasticity. Maize yield is strongly heteroscedastic — high-input plots vary more than low-input plots — so an adaptive width is not a luxury here but a requirement if the interval is to be useful at both ends of the management range. The approach still delivers marginal coverage, and it depends on the quantile model being adequate, which is a real constraint on a panel with seven countries.

Meinshausen [5] offers the alternative route: quantile regression forests read quantiles from the empirical distribution held in the leaves of an ensemble, so one model produces both the prediction and the interval. For the base project the attraction is economy — no separate calibration set is needed, which matters when a target country contributes few rows — but there is no finite-sample coverage guarantee, only an approximation that improves with the number of trees. Choosing between [4] and [5] is therefore a choice between a guarantee that needs data and an approximation that needs none.

Breiman [6] is included because it defines the object whose uncertainty is being quantified. The random forest is the point learner in the base project, and the paper's contribution to this survey is negative but essential: it makes no uncertainty statement at all. Every later step in the base project exists to supply the statement that the point model cannot give.

Barber et al. [7] move beyond exchangeability and give coverage-gap bounds that hold under a measurable distribution shift. This is the theoretical hinge of the survey. It converts the question “does our guarantee survive the move to one country?” from a matter of assertion into a quantity that can in principle be computed and reported. The limitation is practical: the bound requires a shift measure that applied studies rarely have, which is why the applied papers in this corpus report nominal coverage and stop.

3.2 Judging the fit: validation design and leakage

Roberts et al. [11] catalogue cross-validation strategies for data with temporal, spatial, hierarchical or phylogenetic structure, and prescribe blocking that mirrors the structure of the prediction task. This is the design the base project's first module implements: hold out whole clusters for the clustered design and whole countries for the transfer design. The paper is about estimating skill honestly, not about intervals, and that limit is precisely why it cannot be the whole answer — an honest split still leaves the calibration question open.

Ploton et al. [12] supply the evidence that the design matters in practice. Comparing random and spatial validation on large-scale mapping models, they find that spatial validation reveals markedly poorer performance than random validation had suggested. For the base project this is the published precedent for the inflation it measures, and the magnitude reported there is consistent with the +0.088 clustered and +2.208 country-holdout inflation observed on the maize panel. The study concerns ecological mapping rather than yield, so it motivates the audit without supplying its thresholds.

Kapoor and Narayanan [13] name the underlying problem and give it a taxonomy: leakage is the use, during training or evaluation, of information unavailable at prediction time, and its recurring forms include duplicate records, temporal leakage and the reuse of test data for model selection. The survey's value to the base project is vocabulary and a checklist, and its relevance to intervals is direct: the calibration set is part of the evaluation. If calibration rows share clusters with test rows, the scores are computed on data the model has effectively seen and the interval is over-confident by construction, even when every individual step follows the recipe correctly. The paper diagnoses; it does not provide an executable audit of a given pipeline, which is what the base project must build.

3.3 Moving the model: applicability, shift and remedy

Meyer and Pebesma [10] propose the dissimilarity index, a scaled distance in predictor space from each target location to the training data, with a threshold beyond which predictions are flagged as outside the area of applicability. It is the standard pre-deployment check in spatial prediction and the one the base project's domain already uses [15]. Its limitation is structural rather than incidental: the index is computed in covariate space, so a target region can sit comfortably inside the support and still have a different yield distribution because management, market access or measurement practice differ. Demonstrating that blind spot is one of the base project's contributions.

Tibshirani et al. [8] provide the first remedy: under covariate shift, weighting the conformity scores by an estimated likelihood ratio restores validity. The method is principled and its failure mode is well understood — the weights concentrate on few calibration points, the effective sample size collapses and the interval becomes unstable. In a seven-country panel, where one country can be an outlier in several covariates at once, this is a live concern rather than a footnote, and the paper does not quantify the stability cost.

Podkopaev and Ramdas [9] treat the complementary case, where the shift is in the outcome distribution, and show that tracking the risk of a deployed model requires watching the score distribution rather than only the covariates. Translated to yield, the implication is that a small labelled sample from the target country, used to estimate a level offset, may be worth more than a large unlabelled sample and a covariate diagnostic. The paper works in a classification and risk-control setting and does not produce regression intervals, but it supplies the hypothesis the base project's fourth module tests.

3.4 The agronomic base

Aramburu Merlos et al. [14] publish the panel itself: 14,773 georeferenced field records of maize management and yield from smallholder farms in seven countries, licensed CC BY 4.0. Its significance is that the base project's claims are tied to a public, reproducible dataset rather than to a private extract, and its limitation is equally relevant: it is observational, unbalanced across countries and years, and duplicated. The duplicates are not a defect of the release but a property of the survey design that any honest audit must handle explicitly.

Aramburu-Merlos et al. [15] analyse the same panel and show how much of the yield gap could be closed by adopting yield-improving practices, applying an area-of-applicability check of the kind proposed in [10]. The paper is the clearest statement of what the domain currently does: a support check before extrapolation, and point accuracy as the measure of success. It reports no prediction intervals and no leakage audit, which is precisely the gap the base project occupies — the domain already accepts that extrapolation must be checked, but checks it only in covariate space and only for the point prediction.

Read across Table I, the pattern is not a gap in any single row but a gap between rows. Each family answers its own question correctly. None reports, for one domain, both the honest point skill and the interval validity, and none states what it would cost to repair the interval. The practitioner's question — is this model safe here, and what would make it safe — falls between the columns, and Fig. 2 makes the omission visible: the fourth column is empty for every source in the corpus.

4. CRITICAL ASSESSMENT OF THE BASE PROJECT

Three strengths hold up under review. The project takes a public dataset rather than a private extract, so its claims are checkable. It separates the two failures that the corpus usually reports together, which is the specific contribution Table I shows to be missing. And it costs the remedy, reporting the labels and interval width required, which is the concern no surveyed source addresses.

Four weaknesses follow from the same review. First, breadth: the project audits one learner on one panel, so its thresholds are calibrated to maize and cannot be claimed portable. Second, the audit inherits the guarantee of whichever construction it wraps; if it uses split conformal it inherits marginal coverage, and reporting a single per-country coverage figure without interval bounds would overstate precision [1], [3]. Third, the project's diagnosis depends on a covariate-space support check whose insufficiency it asserts; asserting it is weaker than measuring the relation between that check and observed coverage loss, which is what [10] and [7] together would allow. Fourth, the project reports no external validation: every number in it comes from one panel, so the claim that the auditor generalises is a design argument, not a result.

The literature makes each weakness addressable rather than fatal. Blocking is standard [11]; coverage-gap bounds give the language for bounded claims [7]; label-shift reasoning gives a defensible diagnosis when covariates fail to explain the loss [9]; and the comparative framing in [12] gives a precedent for reporting inflation as a measured quantity rather than an assertion.

5. PROPOSED IMPROVEMENTS AND THEIR FEASIBILITY

The survey supports four improvements, shown in Fig. 3 as an audit that reports its four steps together for one domain. Each is feasible with the corpus already reviewed, and each is scoped so that a negative result is still informative.

Check the split before anything else. The auditor should report the inflation between random and blocked designs as its first output, because a model that fails here cannot be salvaged by any interval method [11], [12]. This is already implemented and produces the +0.088 and +2.208 figures quoted in Section 2.

Separate skill from calibration. Report point transfer and per-country coverage as two numbers, with the coverage figure accompanied by an interval so that the reader can see how much evidence stands behind it [1], [3]. Reporting coverage alone invites the over-reading that a single number prevents.

Diagnose which shift is responsible. Compare the covariate-space support verdict with the observed coverage loss; where the support check passes and coverage fails, the shift is in the outcome distribution and a level-adjusted calibration is indicated [9], [10]. This comparison is the project's sharpest claim because it tests the domain's standard practice rather than merely applying it.

Prescribe and cost the remedy. For a level-adjusted recalibration, report the labels required in the target country and the interval width obtained at that budget [4], [9]. A remedy costing two thousand labels per country is not a remedy for an extension service, and stating the number converts a statistical correction into an operational choice. Feasibility here is bounded by the panel: a country with few rows can support the estimate but not a tight one, and the report must say so.

6. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

The three strands surveyed here are individually mature and jointly incomplete. Interval construction is settled enough to apply routinely; evaluation design is known to inflate reported skill when structure is ignored; and transport is handled either by a covariate-space support check or by a reweighting scheme whose failure mode is known. What the corpus does not contain is an instrument that puts the three together for one domain, states which is failing, and costs the repair. That absence is the gap the base project addresses.

Five directions follow, in order of tractability. First, measure the relation between a covariate-space support check and observed coverage loss, so that the diagnostic blind spot becomes a quantified statement rather than an assertion. Second, report coverage with interval bounds for every country, so that small-sample country results are read correctly. Third, establish the label budget curve — coverage restored against labels required — for level-adjusted recalibration on this panel. Fourth, extend the audit to a second crop or a second panel to test whether its thresholds are portable. Fifth, integrate the audit into a reporting artefact that a non-specialist can act on, since the value of a readiness verdict lies in its being read.

Each direction is modest on its own. Together they convert a literature of separate guarantees into a single defensible statement about whether a yield-interval model may be deployed in a given region, which is the question the base project exists to answer.
