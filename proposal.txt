Abstract
This study will test whether a prespecified combination of environmental and management similarities improves maize-yield prediction intervals under geographic holdout in central Zambia. The primary source is the One Acre Fund dataset, whose version-v2 documentation reports 861 central Zambia observations within 14,773 quality-controlled records collected across five African regions during 2016–2022 (Aramburu Merlos et al., 2024). A compulsory feasibility gate will establish the usable regional sample and whether location provenance supports the intended validation.
A common Random Forest predictor will support global split-conformal intervals and environment-only, management-only and composite kernel-weighted interval adaptations. Quantile Regression Forests provide a separate conditional-quantile baseline. A published calibrated-localisation benchmark will distinguish the proposed representation from an uncalibrated weighting heuristic. Empirical spatial coverage, width, interval score, unbounded intervals and calibration-support diagnostics will be reported together. The proposed contribution is an application-specific adaptation and controlled robustness evaluation, with novelty conditional on review. The task is retrospective end-of-season estimation, not prospective forecasting or causal agronomic inference.
1. Problem, aim and scope
1.1 Motivation
The dataset combines measured maize yield with management reports and derived environmental information, providing a basis for investigating heterogeneous prediction uncertainty (Aramburu Merlos et al., 2024). The central question is not whether environmental and management variables affect yield causally, but whether their joint similarity provides more useful uncertainty intervals for geographically separated observations than either feature family alone.
Split conformal prediction has marginal coverage under exchangeability, not universal coverage for every field or subgroup. Plain test-centred local weighting can fail even marginally; calibrated or randomly localised algorithms address different versions of this problem (Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023; Guan, 2023; Hore and Foygel Barber, 2024, sections 2–3). This distinction determines the method labels and permissible claims throughout this proposal.
1.2 Aim and research questions
Aim: develop and reproducibly evaluate a management-and-environment similarity adaptation for maize-yield uncertainty in an audited central Zambia case.
RQ1: Does the audited dataset support geographically separated proper-training, calibration and test partitions?
RQ2: Does composite similarity improve the coverage–width trade-off compared with global, environmental-only and management-only intervals?
RQ3: Do any gains survive a calibrated-localisation benchmark and reasonable spatial-separation sensitivities?
RQ4: Do low calibration support and wide intervals identify unreliable predictions?
1.3 Boundary and intended use
The primary case is central Zambia, not all Zambia or Southern Africa. The observation unit is a field-season record, subject to confirmation of identifiers and repeat visits. The prediction cutoff is harvest/end of season; the analysis does not claim pre-harvest forecasting. Confirm the target column, yield units, moisture basis and measurement aggregation before converting to tonnes per hectare. Coordinate-based validation requires actual location provenance, not merely numeric latitude/longitude.
Intended users are agricultural analysts and extension-planning researchers. No farmer-income increase, prevented loss, insurance readiness, national representativeness or Zimbabwean validity is claimed. Study conclusions concern predictive associations and the observed validation setting, not causal effects of changing fertiliser or planting date.

3. Data and measurement
3.1 Verified published documentation
The Zenodo record identifies version v2, published 6 May 2024, licensed CC BY 4.0. It reports 14,773 post-quality-control observations, including 861 in central Zambia. Yield, density and row spacing were measured in two randomly placed 36 m² boxes at harvest, avoiding edges. Overall location provenance is 70% field, 21% nearby town and 9% district; the latter two were used for climate retrieval (Aramburu Merlos et al., 2024). These are verified documentation claims, not independently reproduced CSV counts.
Management practices and adversities were farmer-reported. About half of all fields belonged to One Acre Fund subscribers. Hybrid maturity and tolerance were assembled from seed catalogues. Soil covariates were retrieved for observations with field-level coordinates. GDD, aridity and temperature seasonality are attributed to WorldClim in the metadata and must not automatically be described as observed weather for each growing season (Aramburu Merlos et al., 2024).
The documentation describes sowing deviation relative to climate-zone/season averages and rainfall windows based on planting/harvest distributions. These supplied derived variables may incorporate information from across an evaluation boundary. Their provenance must be reviewed explicitly; precomputed does not automatically mean leakage-free. The rainfall source narrative says CHIRP while its table links CHIRPS, and phase labels differ between narrative and table. Use neutral “provided seasonal rainfall summaries” until the underlying product and temporal windows are resolved (Aramburu Merlos et al., 2024).

4. Validation and models
4.1 Frozen spatial design
Use three to five outer spatial-group folds only if audited support permits. Keep repeated fields/farms together. Within each outer remainder allocate distinct groups to proper training and calibration; use inner proper-training splits for tuning. All methods share outer test records, exclusions and training/calibration partitions. Spatial blocking and buffered leave-one-location-out are different procedures, not interchangeable names (Roberts et al., 2017; Valavi et al., 2019).
The primary experiment is spatial-group holdout. Add a prespecified exclusion buffer only when support remains adequate; otherwise retain the unbuffered spatial design with its limitations or seek a scope change. Select group construction and plausible separation distances using coordinates, agronomic context and training-only diagnostics, never whichever test outcome looks best. Report train/calibration/test counts and buffer losses per fold. Neither blocking nor buffering establishes exchangeability.

4.3 Distance and weighted threshold
Encode mixed predictors with proper-training transformations. Define each block distance as the mean of prespecified feature contributions, so block dimension alone does not set influence. Use scaled squared differences for numeric features and prespecified mismatch contributions for categorical features. Keep within-block weights equal unless a different rule is frozen on inner data. Audit redundant rainfall summaries so they are not inadvertently overweighted.
Composite localizer: S(x,x_i) = exp[−dE(x,x_i)²/(2hE²) − dM(x,x_i)²/(2hM²)], with positive bandwidths selected from a small frozen grid on inner data. C1 and C2 omit the other term. Residual score: a_i = |y_i−f(x_i)|. Set w_i = S(x,x_i), w_test = S(x,x) = 1, D = sum_i w_i + 1, p_i = w_i/D, p_test = 1/D.
For C1–C3 define q(x) = Q_(1−alpha)[sum_i p_i delta_(a_i) + p_test delta_(+infinity)], and interval [f(x)−q(x), f(x)+q(x)], with alpha = 0.10. Q is the left quantile, the smallest value whose cumulative mass reaches 1−alpha. This is the baseLCP construction, not Guan’s calibrated algorithm. Hore and Foygel Barber (2024, section 2) explicitly explain why baseLCP may fail marginal coverage despite its self-weight/infinity atom. C5 must instead follow the published level adjustment and score inversion (Guan, 2023).
C0 uses k = ceil((n_cal+1)(1−alpha)); if k&gt;n_cal, return infinity. Preserve infinity in all methods when finite mass is insufficient. Do not cap at the largest residual or omit failed cases. At 90% and unit self-weight, sum_i w_i≥9 is necessary for a finite threshold when calibration scores are finite; this algebraic condition is not a statistical-stability guarantee. Equal kernel weights must reproduce C0.
4.4 Calibration-support plan
Define ESS = (sum_i w_i)²/sum_i w_i² on calibration weights, with zero for no calibration mass. It measures weight concentration, not independent information or a coverage guarantee. Report raw calibration count, ESS, maximum normalised weight and self-mass at each test point. Small bandwidths can cause insufficient effective support and infinity thresholds (Hore and Foygel Barber, 2024, section 2.1).

5. Evaluation and inference
Primary metrics are empirical coverage, mean/median width, interval score and unbounded-interval frequency. Report MAE and RMSE in confirmed yield units. Interval score at error rate alpha is (U−L)+(2/alpha)(L−y)1(y&lt;L)+(2/alpha)(y−U)1(y&gt;U) (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). Infinity outputs make overall mean width/score infinite; finite-only summaries are supplementary and must show their denominator.
Show coverage successes/total and every fold’s support and buffer losses. Provide pooled observation-weighted and equal-fold summaries, noting their different estimands. With only a few spatial groups, use descriptive paired block differences rather than treating all fields or folds as independent replicates. A block bootstrap is optional only when enough defensible resampling units exist; no confidence interval is represented as fixing dependence automatically.
Primary comparisons are C3−C0, C3−C1, C3−C2 and C3−C5. The exploratory engineering target is ≥5% narrower width than C0 with ≤3 percentage-point coverage loss; additionally report absolute coverage versus 90%. Meeting this relative target when both methods materially under-cover is not a successful reliability result. No p-value or positive result is promised.
Report core versus expanded predictors, one alternate spatial separation, low-support/fallback frequency and a second nominal level only if support/time permits. Examine error by support/width quartile and retention curves with rules fixed on inner data. Avoid small-subgroup rankings; disclose counts. Null results, poor spatial transfer, severe undercoverage and infeasibility are legitimate findings.
